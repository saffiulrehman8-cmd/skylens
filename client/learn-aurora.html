Space Weather
& Aurora

Solar wind, geomagnetic storms, and the physics behind the northern lights.

Related Topics

Atmospheric Optics
Global Circulation
Climate Anomalies

The aurora begins with the solar wind — a constant stream of charged particles flowing from the sun. When a coronal mass ejection (CME) reaches Earth, it compresses the magnetosphere and funnels particles along magnetic field lines toward the poles, where they collide with oxygen and nitrogen in the upper atmosphere.

Those collisions excite the gas atoms, which release the excess energy as light. Oxygen around 100–150km produces the familiar green glow; rarer red comes from oxygen higher up, above 200km. Nitrogen near the lower edge of the aurora produces blue and purple. Strong geomagnetic storms push this display — normally confined near the poles — to far lower latitudes than usual.

🌌 Gannon Storm (2024): The strongest geomagnetic storm in over two decades pushed the aurora as far south as Mexico — visible to more people than any space weather event in recorded history.
