Local Wind
Systems

Sea breezes and mountain winds — the small-scale airflows a weather map won't show.

Related Topics

Global Circulation
Monsoon Systems
Heat Waves & Urban Heat

Not all wind comes from global circulation — much of what people actually feel day to day is driven locally. A sea breeze forms because land heats faster than water: rising warm air over land draws cooler ocean air inland during the afternoon. At night the pattern reverses into a gentler land breeze, as land cools faster than the sea.

Mountains create their own cycle: anabatic winds flow upslope by day as sun-warmed valley air rises, while katabatic winds sink downslope after dark as cooled, denser air drains toward the valley floor. The most dramatic local wind is the Foehn (Alps) or Chinook (Rockies) — air that dries out climbing one side of a mountain range then compresses and warms sharply descending the other, sometimes raising temperatures tens of degrees within hours.

🌡 World Record: A Chinook wind in Loma, Montana raised the temperature 103°F in just 24 hours on January 14–15, 1972 — from -54°F to 49°F — still the largest 24-hour temperature swing ever recorded, per Guinness World Records.
