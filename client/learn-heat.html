Heat Waves &
Urban Heat

Why concrete cities run hotter than the countryside around them.

Related Topics

Severe Storms
Local Wind Systems

A heat wave is a prolonged period of abnormally hot weather, typically defined relative to a location's normal climate rather than a fixed global threshold. Extended heat is especially dangerous because the human body struggles to cool itself once both air temperature and humidity stay elevated for consecutive days, particularly overnight when the body normally recovers.

Cities intensify this through the Urban Heat Island effect: dense asphalt and concrete absorb solar energy and slowly re-release it as heat, while lost vegetation means less natural cooling through shade and evaporation. Waste heat from vehicles and air conditioning adds further warming, and narrow "urban canyons" between tall buildings trap heat that would otherwise disperse.

🏙 First Discovery: Luke Howard documented the urban heat island effect as early as 1833, comparing air temperatures in central London to its surrounding countryside — over 150 years before the term became common.
